Spring Food Plot Planting Guide: Best Late-Spring Crops for Deer in 2026

May 19, 2026 · 6 min read

If you only plant food plots in the fall, you're leaving a huge chunk of your land's wildlife potential on the table. Spring and early summer plantings are what build antlers, support lactating does, and pull deer onto your property before bow season even starts. The problem is that "plant something in the spring" is the level of advice most articles give you, and that's not actually useful when you're standing in front of a tractor wondering what's going to survive a Midwestern May.

Here's a practical breakdown of what to plant, when to plant it, and which crops are actually worth the time and seed money for a small-to-mid-size property in 2026.

Why Spring Plots Matter More Than People Think

Late spring and early summer is when does are coming off their hardest stretch of the year. They've spent the winter on whatever forage was left over, then dropped fawns, and now they need protein in serious quantities to make milk. Bucks coming out of winter need to rebuild body mass and start putting energy into antler growth. The crops you plant in May and June are what those deer eat during the most nutritionally demanding eight weeks of their year.

This is also when you can shape where deer spend their time. A property with no spring food but good fall plots will pull deer in October. A property with both will hold deer year-round, which means more daylight movement during the season and better fawn survival rates.

The Two Crops That Do The Most Work

If you only plant two things this spring, plant soybeans and clover. Together they cover everything you need: high-protein browse from late May through frost, season-long attraction, and a perennial component that comes back year after year.

Soybeans are the single most attractive summer crop in most of the country. Deer hammer them from the moment the cotyledons emerge until the first frost ends the green-leaf stage. The catch is browse pressure. On small properties with high deer density, a half-acre of soybeans will get mowed to the ground inside of two weeks. If you've got fewer than five acres of summer plot total and a healthy herd, you either need to fence the beans until they're tall enough to survive, plant Eagle Seed forage beans (which regrow after browse), or accept that they're going to be a salad bar instead of a maturing crop.

Clover is the perennial workhorse. A well-established clover plot will produce forage for four to seven years with minimal maintenance — just mow it once or twice a season to keep it from going to seed and to knock back competition. Crimson clover germinates fastest and is great for spring overseeding into existing plots. White clover (Durana, Ladino, or a blend) is the long-term play. Plant white clover in early spring with a nurse crop of oats or winter wheat, mow it once it's established, and you've got a plot that will outperform almost anything else on a per-dollar basis.

Planning Your Spring Planting Window

Timing is the most underrated variable. Most people plant either too early — when the soil is still cold and seeds rot — or too late, after the spring rains have stopped and germination becomes a coin flip.

Soil Temperature Beats The Calendar

Forget the dates on the back of the seed bag. The number that matters is soil temperature at four inches deep. Most warm-season annuals — soybeans, sunflowers, sorghum, buckwheat — want to see consistent 60-degree soil before you plant. Cool-season crops like oats, peas, clover, and brassicas can go in once you're holding 45 to 50 degrees consistently.

A $15 soil thermometer pays for itself the first season. Take readings in the morning at the actual plot location, not from your truck thermometer. South-facing plots warm up two weeks earlier than north-facing ones, and that gap matters.

Rain Windows

You want your seed in the ground three to seven days before a rain event. Too close to the rain and you risk seed wash; too far and the seed sits exposed to birds and dries out. Check the 10-day forecast and pick your planting day backwards from a reliable-looking weather window.

A Practical Spring Mix

For a small property where you're managing 1 to 5 acres of plot, here's a mix that has worked across most of the eastern half of the country:

  • 60% of your acreage in soybeans — either a forage variety if you have browse pressure, or standard ag beans if you've got the acreage to absorb the early hits.
  • 25% in established or new clover plots — these are your insurance policy when other plots fail.
  • 10% in sunflowers — these double as a screening crop along plot edges and as fall dove and turkey attraction.
  • 5% in buckwheat — fast germination, smothers weeds, fixes nitrogen, and turkeys love it.

This isn't a magic formula. It's a starting point. If you've got a soybean problem because of browse pressure, lean harder on clover. If you're managing turkey numbers as much as deer, increase the buckwheat and sunflowers. The mix adapts to your land.

Soil Prep Without Overcomplicating It

You don't need a no-till drill, a brand-new tractor, or a soil scientist. You need a soil test, lime if your pH is below 6.0, and a way to disturb the surface enough to get seed-to-soil contact.

The Soil Test Conversation

Every state extension service runs soil tests for around $20 per sample. Send one in for each plot location, separated by soil type. The results tell you exactly how much lime and fertilizer to apply, and skipping this step is the single biggest reason food plots fail. A clover plot on unlimed acidic soil will limp along for one season and die. The same plot on properly limed soil will produce for five years.

Lime doesn't work overnight. If your test comes back recommending two tons per acre, get it on the ground in early spring or, better, the fall before. Pelletized lime is more expensive per ton but works faster than ag lime and is easier to handle if you don't have a spreader truck coming in.

Equipment Realities

For small plots under an acre, an ATV-mounted disc and a hand spreader will get the job done. For one to five acres, a small tractor with a tiller or a disc-and-cultipacker setup is the sweet spot. Anything bigger than that and renting a no-till drill from your county becomes worth the money — it gives you better seed depth control, cuts down on weed pressure, and dramatically improves germination rates in dry years.

Weed Pressure And Maintenance

The number one reason new food plots fail is weed competition. Pigweed, ragweed, marestail, and crabgrass will outpace anything you plant if you don't have a plan. There are three workable approaches.

First, you can spray glyphosate or another non-selective herbicide two weeks before planting to kill the existing weed seed bank. Second, you can choose Roundup Ready soybean varieties and spray over-the-top after the beans are up. Third, you can mow aggressively and accept lower yields in exchange for not using chemicals.

For clover specifically, the maintenance is mostly mowing. Cut clover when it gets above 10 inches tall, and don't cut shorter than six inches. This keeps the clover competitive against grass invaders and stimulates new growth. A single mowing in mid-summer and another in late summer is usually enough.

Getting The Most Out Of Trail Cameras This Season

Once your plots are in, your trail cameras tell you whether your strategy is working. Set cameras on plot entry trails rather than in the middle of the plot — you'll get cleaner pictures, less battery drain from constant triggers, and better data on which deer are actually using the area versus which are just browsing the edges.

Time-lapse mode is underused. Setting a camera to take a picture every 15 minutes during the last two hours of daylight gives you a frame-by-frame record of plot use that motion triggers miss entirely. You'll find out fast whether mature bucks are using your plots at last light, which is the data that actually informs hunting decisions in October.

Building A Year-Round System

The properties that consistently produce mature bucks aren't the ones with the biggest food plots. They're the ones where spring, summer, and fall food sources work together, where the deer never have a hungry month. A solid spring plot rotation makes your fall plots more effective because the deer were already there in July. Get the spring right and the rest of your season gets easier.
