How to Set Up Mock Scrapes for Trail Camera Buck Inventory in 2026

May 26, 2026 · 8 min read

If you run trail cameras and you are not running mock scrapes in front of them, you are missing about a third of the bucks that walk your property. That is not an exaggeration. A camera on a random trail captures whatever happens to wander by. A camera on a well-made mock scrape gets the same deer, but it also gets the bucks that travel the edges, the cruising satellites during the pre-rut, and the older, smarter bucks that avoid open ground. Mock scrapes turn a passive camera into a magnet.

The other reason to learn this skill is that mock scrapes work year-round. They are not just an October trick. Bucks use community scrapes from August all the way into late January, and the right setup in summer will inventory every shooter in your area months before opening day. Here is how to build them properly, where to put them, and how to keep them hot all season.

What a mock scrape actually is

A scrape is two things working together. The pawed ground gets the attention, but the licking branch hanging above it is where the real communication happens. Deer rub their preorbital, forehead, and nasal glands on that branch every time they visit. They read every other deer that has been there. The pawed dirt below is the bulletin board. The branch is the message.

A mock scrape is just the same thing made by you. If you give them a believable licking branch over fresh dirt in a believable spot, they will adopt it within forty-eight hours, often within twelve. Bucks have used these communal signposts forever. They do not care that you made it as long as it makes sense to them.

Step 1: Pick a believable location

This is where most people go wrong. They put a mock scrape wherever they want to put a camera, not where deer actually want to communicate. The result is a clean scrape that nothing touches. To find a believable spot, walk your property and look for the natural travel funnels:

  • Inside corners of fields, where the cover comes to a point and travel naturally pinches through.
  • Saddles between ridges, any low spot where deer would cross to save energy.
  • Trail intersections in the timber with a hub-and-spoke pattern.
  • Field edges thirty to fifty yards inside the woods, where bucks cruise without exposing themselves.
  • Existing rubs from past seasons, which tell you a buck already considered this his bedroom.

If you see an existing scrape from last year, use it. Old scrapes get reopened every year by the same family of bucks. You are not creating anything new, you are reactivating a known address.

Step 2: Find or build the licking branch

The licking branch sits about five feet off the ground, give or take. It should be sturdy enough to take a buck thrashing it but flexible enough to bend when he works it with his face. Hardwoods like cedar, oak, hickory, and persimmon all work great because the bark holds scent for weeks. Pine works but loses scent faster.

If you have a perfect funnel but no overhanging branch, you can wire one in. Cut a fresh limb from somewhere else on your property, ideally from a tree of the same species, and zip-tie it to a higher branch so that the tip hangs at the right height. Use black zip ties and tuck them up where deer will not see them. Within a day, the branch smells like the surrounding woods and they accept it as native.

Step 3: Make the scrape itself

Clear an area about three feet wide directly under the licking branch. Use a stick, the back of a shovel, or a dedicated scrape rake. Get down to bare dirt and remove leaves, sticks, and debris. The patch does not need to be huge, but it does need to be obvious and freshly disturbed. Bucks notice the contrast.

One trick that speeds adoption: stomp the bare dirt with your boot a few times. Deer perceive ground disturbance as evidence of activity. A scrape that looks like a deer pawed it yesterday gets reopened faster than one that looks perfectly raked.

Step 4: Scent it correctly

You do not need to dump a bottle of dominant buck urine to make a mock scrape work. In fact, that often backfires in summer when bucks are bachelored up and have no interest in territorial fights. What you want is something that says "another deer was here recently" without picking a specific social signal.

For pre-season inventory work in July, August, and early September, a few drops of pure preorbital scent or a synthetic interdigital scent on the licking branch is enough. As you move into October, switch to a quality territorial scrape scent. During pre-rut and rut, dominant buck urine on the dirt and tarsal scent on the branch will trigger investigation from any mature buck in the area.

Refresh the scent every two to three weeks, or every time you swap cards on the camera. Old scent loses its punch fast in heat and rain.

Step 5: Set up the camera correctly

Camera placement around a mock scrape matters as much as the scrape itself. Get this wrong and you photograph a lot of antler tips with cropped-off bodies. Here is the rule of thumb that has worked for me on dozens of setups:

  • Distance: ten to fifteen feet from the scrape, never closer than eight.
  • Height: chest high on the deer, not chest high on you. About thirty inches off the ground.
  • Angle: aim across the scrape, not straight at it. A quartering angle catches the buck's whole body, the rack, and the action at the branch all in one frame.
  • Direction: point north or south whenever possible to avoid sun washout at dawn and dusk.

If you are running a cellular trail camera, use a low-resolution thumbnail setting for daily images so you do not blow through data, but keep the SD card recording at full resolution for the year-end review.

How to scout a buck inventory

The point of all this is to know your deer before opening day. A solid pre-season inventory tells you how many shooters you have, what their travel patterns look like, and which stand sites will give you the best chance at each one. Here is a system that works.

Pick four to six spots

Spread your scrapes across the property to cover different bedding areas. Putting them too close together just photographs the same bucks at multiple locations. Aim for one mock scrape per forty to sixty acres of huntable cover.

Run them from late July through mid-October

By the time velvet starts to peel in early September, you will have clean broadside photos of nearly every buck living on or near your land. Bachelor groups still travel together at this point, which makes counting easier.

Catalog every unique buck

Build a simple folder per buck. Use the most distinctive feature for the name, kicker, drop tine, split G2, whatever. Note the date, time, weather, and which mock scrape it was. Patterns emerge fast once you have thirty days of data. You will see which bucks are crepuscular, which are nocturnal, and which ones lock onto a specific scrape as a primary signpost.

Common mistakes to avoid

Plenty of mock scrapes get built and nothing happens. The reasons are almost always the same:

  • Over-handling the area. Wear rubber boots, gloves, and a scent-control top. Be in and out in fifteen minutes.
  • Bad licking branch height. Too high and only the tallest bucks reach it. Too low and does work it instead of bucks, which sometimes spooks the resident buck.
  • No water nearby. Mock scrapes near a creek crossing, pond, or seep get hit twice as often.
  • Cheap scent. The bargain bin synthetics smell like chemicals to deer. Buy from one of the reputable scent makers and refresh it.
  • Camera too close. A buck that hears the shutter or sees the IR flash gets educated quickly and avoids the spot.

Late-season bonus play

Once gun season ends and most hunters have moved on, mock scrapes can pull surviving bucks back into the open during the secondary rut and right before they shed. If you have winter food still standing, set a fresh mock scrape on the downwind edge of it and refresh the licking branch. You will get some of the cleanest year-end photos of the season, which tells you exactly which bucks made it through and what you are working with for next year.

Mock scrapes are one of those small skills that compound. The first year you might catch a dozen bucks on camera you would have otherwise missed. The third year you have a complete history of the herd, you know where the old bucks live, and you start setting up stands with conviction instead of hope. That is the difference between hunting and guessing.
