How to Do Timber Stand Improvement (TSI) for Better Deer Habitat in 2026

June 18, 2026 · 7 min read

Most hunting properties have too many trees. That sounds backwards until you walk into a mature, closed-canopy woodlot and notice what is missing: there is nothing growing at deer level. The canopy has shaded out every blade of forage and every stem of cover, leaving a clean, parklike forest floor that looks beautiful and holds almost no deer. Timber stand improvement, or TSI, is the practice of deliberately killing or cutting selected trees so sunlight reaches the ground and the woods explode with the browse and bedding cover that deer actually use.

Done well, TSI is one of the highest-impact habitat projects available to a landowner, and it costs little more than a chainsaw, a bottle of herbicide, and your time. Done carelessly, it can drop a tree on you or release a thicket of worthless invasive brush. This guide covers how to do it right in 2026.

Why Sunlight Is the Whole Game

A deer eats at ground level, browsing the tender tips of woody stems, forbs, and brambles within about five feet of the dirt. In a mature forest, the dominant trees intercept nearly all the sunlight before it reaches that zone. Foresters call it a closed canopy, and underneath it the understory starves.

When you remove some of those dominant trees, you open holes in the canopy. Sunlight pours through, dormant seeds in the soil germinate, stump sprouts erupt, and within a single growing season the area transforms from bare ground into a tangle of green forage and screening cover. The goal of TSI is to create that condition on purpose, in the right places, while favoring the trees worth keeping.

Deciding Which Trees to Keep and Which to Kill

This is the part that paralyzes people, so use a simple framework. You are sorting every tree into one of three buckets: keepers, cuts, and crop trees you release.

Trees Worth Keeping

Favor anything that feeds deer or holds long-term value. Mast producers are the priority: white oaks, red oaks, beech, persimmon, and any fruit trees. A mature, heavy-bearing white oak is worth more to your herd than a dozen low-value stems around it, so keep it and remove its competitors to give the crown room to expand.

Trees Worth Removing

Target low-value species that occupy space without paying rent. In much of the country that means red maple, elm, sweetgum, hornbeam, beech thickets, and any non-mast hardwood crowding a more valuable tree. Also remove diseased, crooked, or damaged stems. The classic technique is the crop tree release: pick a desirable tree, then kill the three or four trees whose crowns touch its crown, freeing it to thrive.

The Two Core Techniques

There are two ways to remove a tree in TSI, and you will use both depending on your goal for that spot.

Hinge Cutting for Instant Cover

Hinge cutting means cutting partway through a small tree, around six to eight inches in diameter, then pushing it over so it falls but stays attached to the stump by a strip of living bark. The tree keeps living horizontally, putting browse at deer height and creating instant horizontal cover. Hinge cutting is the fastest way to build a bedding area, because a few dozen hinged stems create a screen and a maze that deer love to bed in.

Hinge cutting is also the most dangerous part of TSI, so respect it. Only hinge healthy trees in the right diameter range, never hinge anything overhead-heavy or leaning hard, always cut at a comfortable waist height, and keep an escape path clear. Make your cut at a slight downward angle and stop while plenty of hinge wood remains, then push the tree away from you with a pole, never by hand near the cut. If a tree is large, dead, or leaning unpredictably, do not hinge it. Fell it or kill it standing instead.

Hack-and-Squirt for Clean Standing Kills

For larger trees, or anywhere you want to avoid the mess and hazard of dropping timber, use the hack-and-squirt method. Make a downward cut into the bark with a hatchet every few inches around the trunk, then squirt a small amount of concentrated glyphosate or triclopyr herbicide into each fresh cut. The tree dies standing over the following weeks, the canopy opens, and the dead snag becomes valuable wildlife habitat for cavity-nesting birds while it slowly falls apart on its own. This is the safest high-volume technique and the one to lean on for trees over about ten inches across.

How Much to Cut

The most common mistake is being too timid. A few scattered cuts barely change the light. As a rule of thumb, aim to remove enough canopy that roughly forty to sixty percent of the ground gets direct sunlight in the treated area. In a thick stand that can mean killing half the stems. It will look aggressive the day you finish and look brilliant one growing season later.

Do not treat your whole property at once. Work in blocks of a few acres, leave untreated travel corridors between them, and stagger the work over several years. That gives you a patchwork of different-aged cover, which holds far more deer than one uniform treatment.

Timing the Work

The best window for the cutting itself is late winter into early spring, before green-up. The weather is cool, the snakes are down, the leaves are off so you can see crowns clearly, and the work is simply more pleasant. For herbicide application, however, the most effective time is late summer into fall when trees are actively moving sugars and herbicide down to their roots, which gives you a more complete kill. Many landowners split the difference: hack-and-squirt in the fall, hinge cutting and felling in late winter.

Controlling What Comes Back

Opening the canopy invites everything to grow, including the plants you do not want. In many regions, invasive species such as autumn olive, bush honeysuckle, and multiflora rose will rush into the sunlight and form a monoculture thicket that crowds out native browse. Plan to spot-spray or cut these invaders during the first two growing seasons after your TSI work. If you skip this step, you can turn a productive native understory into a worthless invasive wall, so budget time for follow-up before you ever start cutting.

Reading the Results

By the end of the first summer you should see a flush of greenbrier, blackberry, raspberry, native forbs, and young woody sprouts in your treated blocks. That is exactly what you want. Deer will start using the new browse almost immediately and the bedding cover within a season or two as it thickens. Trail cameras placed on the edges of treated blocks will show you how quickly the animals respond.

Pairing TSI With the Rest of Your Habitat Plan

TSI is most powerful when it works alongside your other habitat efforts rather than in isolation. The bedding thickets you create with hinge cutting become far more valuable when they sit near a food source, so think about how a treated block connects to a food plot, a crop field edge, or a stand of mast-producing oaks. Deer want to bed close to food and travel short, secure distances between the two, and TSI lets you place that secure cover exactly where you want deer to live.

Use your cutting to shape movement, too. By leaving uncut strips and felling trees to block certain routes, you can nudge deer along predictable travel corridors that funnel past a stand location. This is the quiet advantage of TSI that many landowners overlook: you are not just growing more food and cover, you are designing where animals move through the property.

A Realistic First-Year Plan

If this all feels like a lot, shrink it. Pick a single two- to three-acre block this year. Walk it in late winter and flag your keeper oaks and fruit trees. Release each keeper by killing the three or four trees crowding its crown with hack-and-squirt. Hinge cut a cluster of small stems in one corner to start a bedding pocket. Then leave it alone through the growing season, spot-spray any invasives that rush in, and watch what happens. One small, well-executed block teaches you more than reading ever will, and it gives you the confidence to scale up across the property in following years.

Tools and Safety Worth the Money

You can do meaningful TSI with a single chainsaw, a hatchet, and a spray bottle, but a few items earn their keep: chainsaw-rated chaps, a helmet with face and ear protection, and cut-resistant gloves are non-negotiable. A felling lever or pole for pushing hinged trees keeps your hands away from the cut. And a partner on site changes a chainsaw accident from a tragedy into a manageable emergency, so try never to run a saw alone deep in the timber.

TSI rewards patience and a willingness to be bolder than feels comfortable. Pick a few acres, release your best oaks, build a bedding thicket with hinge cuts, kill the junk standing with herbicide, and come back next year to a woodlot that finally works for the deer that live on it.
