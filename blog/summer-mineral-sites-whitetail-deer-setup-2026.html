How to Set Up Summer Mineral Sites for Whitetail Deer in 2026

May 24, 2026 · 7 min read

Late May through August is when bucks grow antler and does grow fawns, and almost every meaningful gram of bone and milk has to come from the soil. On most farms, the soil is short on the trace minerals that drive that growth. A handful of well-placed mineral sites fixes the deficit, draws deer into predictable patterns, and turns your trail camera SD cards into a summer-long inventory you can actually act on at the start of season.

Done right, a mineral site is one of the cheapest, most effective improvements you can make to a hunting property. Done wrong, it is a salty mud puddle that wastes fifty dollars of bagged product and tells the deer nothing about your management. The difference is mostly in the details below.

When to Start Putting Out Minerals

The trigger is green-up, not the calendar. As soon as the buds break and the woods take on that yellow-green haze, deer start craving sodium. Spring forage is high in potassium and water and low in salt; their bodies want to balance it out. That is when minerals get hit hardest.

In most of the whitetail range that window opens between late March and early May. In 2026, with a slightly cooler April across the Midwest and Southeast, many farms saw peak usage start a couple of weeks later than normal. A safe rule: put out your first round when you can see fresh leaves on the hardwoods. Refresh it every four to six weeks through August.

By late August, antlers are nearly finished mineralizing and use drops off sharply. There is no harm in leaving the site, but you do not need to keep dumping product into it.

Picking the Right Location

Three rules cover almost every good mineral spot.

1. Near water, not in it

Find a draw or bench within a hundred yards of a creek, pond, or seep. Deer pair drinking with mineral and bedding, so a site that fits that natural pattern gets used twice as much as one stuck in a random clearing. Avoid putting the actual lick in a wet bottom; you want the salts to leach into a defined, well-drained spot, not wash off in the next thunderstorm.

2. On the edge of bedding, not in it

The best mineral spots sit at the seam between bedding cover and a travel route to feed. Doe groups will swing through twice a day, and mature bucks will check the site every few days at night and in low light. Putting it in the middle of bedding pressures the cover; putting it in the open feeding field gets you mostly fawns and yearlings on camera.

3. Where wind serves you in October

Think a season ahead. The trees you slop with mineral in May are the trees you may be hunting in November. Pick spots where the prevailing fall wind carries scent away from likely bedding, and the camera approach path does not cross the bed-to-feed line. Future-you will be grateful.

What Goes in a Good Mineral Mix

Deer need a lot more than just salt. A balanced mineral targets the macro- and micro-elements that drive antler and lactation: calcium and phosphorus first, then magnesium, sodium, copper, zinc, manganese, and selenium. The ratio that matches a whitetail's blood chemistry runs about 2:1 calcium to phosphorus.

You have three reasonable paths to that mix.

Bagged commercial mineral

Brands like Trophy Rock, Big & J, Whitetail Institute 30-06, and Lucky Buck all formulate for whitetail. They are convenient, consistent, and tested. Expect to pay roughly thirty to forty-five dollars per fifty-pound bag in 2026, which covers one site for the summer.

DIY two-bag mix

The classic homemade recipe is one part trace mineral salt to one part dicalcium phosphate, mixed together. Trace mineral salt is sold at any farm store as a livestock product, usually for under fifteen dollars a fifty-pound bag. Dicalcium phosphate runs about thirty dollars per fifty-pound bag at a feed mill. Together you get a hundred pounds of balanced mineral for under fifty dollars — roughly a third of the cost of branded product.

Mineral block plus loose top-dress

A block lasts longer and weathers slowly, which is helpful on a property you visit only every few weeks. Pair it with a small ring of loose mineral around the base so deer can dig and ingest fine product. The block holds the spot in the dirt; the loose stuff drives the heavy usage.

How to Build the Site

Pick a tree with workable soil at its base. A stump works fine too. Then:

  • Clear a circle roughly four feet across down to bare dirt with a hoe or mattock
  • Break up the top two or three inches so minerals can leach in
  • Pour out four to ten pounds of mix, depending on herd size
  • Work it into the loose soil with the back of the hoe
  • Save a pound to dust on top as an attractant in the first few weeks

Some old-timers swear by burying a fence-post-sized hole packed with mineral. It works, but the topsoil approach lets deer dig naturally and creates the obvious pawed-out lick that signals the site is hot. Either way, the salt and minerals will keep working in the soil for at least one full season; many sites stay active for years.

Refresh Schedule and How Much to Use

For a property running roughly one deer per twenty acres, four to six pounds of mineral per site every four to six weeks through summer is plenty. Heavier deer density or bigger groups can take ten to fifteen pounds per refresh. The site itself will tell you: if the dirt is pawed two feet deep and the loose product is gone in three weeks, scale up. If product is still sitting on top after a month, scale down.

A reasonable summer plan for a 100-acre tract: three to four sites, refreshed three times, runs about 100 to 150 pounds of product. That is roughly $60 to $100 in DIY mix.

Using Trail Cameras Over a Mineral Site

A summer mineral site is the single best place on the farm to build a buck inventory. Deer linger, return, and rotate through the same patch of dirt, which means you photograph almost the entire local population over six to eight weeks.

Setup tips

  • Mount the camera ten to twelve feet from the lick at chest height of a deer, on a tree the morning sun does not hit directly
  • Aim slightly down toward the bare dirt, not across it
  • Use a one-minute trigger interval; mineral sites generate dozens of photos per visit otherwise
  • Set video clips of fifteen to twenty seconds on cellular cameras — you will see antler from multiple angles
  • Pull cards or download remotely at midday when deer activity is lowest

Cell cameras with solar panels have gotten cheap enough in 2026 that a four-camera summer setup costs less than a single guided hunt. The data is worth far more.

Reading the inventory

Once mineral pictures start rolling in, build a simple list of mature bucks. Note antler character, body size, and which sites each buck visits. By late July, antlers are detailed enough to identify individuals reliably. By mid-August, you can pencil in a target list of three or four bucks and know roughly which corner of the property holds each one.

Legal Considerations by State

Mineral and bait rules vary by state, and several have tightened in the last few years because of chronic wasting disease (CWD) concerns. Before you scoop a single shovel of dirt, check your state regulations.

As a general guide for 2026:

  • Year-round mineral is legal in most of the Midwest and Plains states, including Missouri, Kansas, Nebraska, and Indiana
  • Summer mineral only is the rule in several states like Michigan, where minerals must be removed a set number of days before season
  • All baiting and minerals are banned in parts of the Northeast and in CWD management zones in Wisconsin, Minnesota, and a handful of others
  • Block-only restrictions apply in a few states where loose mineral is treated as bait

Check the actual current statute on your state wildlife agency's site. Rules update yearly. A five-minute read keeps a $50 mineral site from turning into a $500 ticket.

A Realistic First-Year Plan

If this is your first time, do not try to perfect it. Pick two or three locations that meet the water-plus-edge rule above. Drop a bagged commercial mineral on each. Hang a camera. Refresh in six weeks. Take pictures. At the end of the summer, you will know which sites work, which deer use them, and which spots are not pulling their weight. Year two, you adjust.

Mineral is one of the few habitat improvements that pays off in the same year you do it. Antlers grow harder, fawns survive better, and your trail camera tells you the story all summer long. Get the first sites in this week if green-up is on, and you will be reading the deer on your farm long before bow season opens.
