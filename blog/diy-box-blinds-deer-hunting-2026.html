How to Build a DIY Box Blind for Deer Hunting in 2026

May 15, 2026 · 8 min read

A good box blind changes everything about how you hunt a piece of property. You stay dry when the weather turns, you stay still when the deer are close, and you can put a young kid or an older hunter in there without spending the morning shivering on a tree stand ladder. The catch is that the price of a decent prefab tower blind has climbed past 2,000 dollars by 2026, and the cheap ones look great in the catalog but leak by year two.

Building your own is the right move if you have a weekend, a half-decent set of tools, and a friend willing to swing the other end of a sheet of plywood. A homemade box blind, done right, lasts 15 to 20 years. The materials run roughly 350 to 600 dollars depending on what is on sale at the lumber yard and whether you upgrade to insulated windows. Here is the build that has been refined over a lot of seasons and a lot of mistakes.

Pick the Spot Before You Pick the Plans

The most expensive blind in the world is one that sits in the wrong place. Deer move differently in November than they did in September, and what looked like an obvious funnel on a summer aerial photo is sometimes 70 yards off the real travel corridor.

Before you cut a single board, do this:

  • Run cell trail cameras for at least three weeks on the spot you are considering. Confirm deer use the area at legal shooting light, not just at 2 a.m.
  • Walk the location with a compass. The prevailing wind during your hunting season needs to be in your face or quartering away from the deer travel direction. A blind set against the wrong wind never gets used.
  • Identify your access route. You should be able to walk in and out without crossing the bedding area or the food source. If the only access is straight through where the deer want to be, pick a different spot.
  • Check for distant disturbances. Neighboring houses, a county road, a power line you cannot see from inside the woods but will hear all night - these matter more than you think when you spend a hundred hours in the blind over a season.

Once you have a spot you trust, stand at it for ten minutes and look at the actual shooting lanes. The window placement on your blind needs to match what you can see, not what you wish you could see.

Dimensions That Actually Work

Most plans you find online were designed for one slim hunter sitting still. Real-world use means a hunter in heavy clothes, a daypack, maybe a small heater, and the occasional second person along for a learning hunt. Build accordingly.

The sweet spot for a one-to-two-person box blind:

  • Floor: 5 feet by 5 feet, or 4 feet by 6 feet if you want a longer line of sight in one direction. Smaller than this and you cannot turn your shoulders for a shot.
  • Wall height: 6 feet 6 inches inside. You want to be able to stand up and stretch, especially on an all-day November sit.
  • Window height: the bottom of the main windows should be 36 to 38 inches off the floor when you are seated. Set them by sitting in a folding chair in the build location and marking where your bow or rifle naturally rests.
  • Window size: 24 inches wide by 10 to 12 inches tall on three sides. One narrower window on the back for situational awareness. Wide horizontal windows give you more flex on shots without giving deer a giant moving silhouette to spot.
  • Tower height: 8 to 10 feet to the floor in open country, 10 to 12 feet at the edge of timber. Higher is not always better. Above 12 feet the geometry of a steep shot angle works against you.

Materials List

This is the list for a 5 x 5 blind on a 10-foot tower. Adjust quantities up or down for your size. Buy the pressure-treated lumber for anything that touches the ground; everything else can be standard SPF.

  • Four pressure-treated 4x4 posts, 12 feet long (legs)
  • Six pressure-treated 2x6, 8 feet (floor framing, ground cross-bracing)
  • Eight 2x4, 8 feet (wall studs, plates, roof rafters)
  • Two 2x4, 10 feet (cross-bracing for the tower)
  • One sheet of 3/4-inch pressure-treated plywood (floor)
  • Four sheets of 1/2-inch exterior-grade plywood (walls and roof)
  • One sheet of 1/2-inch plywood (interior gussets and shelf)
  • One roll of 30 lb roofing felt and one box of 1.25-inch roofing nails
  • One bundle of architectural shingles, or a 4x8 sheet of corrugated metal roofing
  • Three pounds of 3-inch exterior screws, two pounds of 1-5/8-inch deck screws
  • Eight 1/2-inch x 5-inch carriage bolts, washers, and nuts for leg-to-floor connections
  • One quart of camo or earth-tone exterior paint
  • Window plexiglass or acrylic - six 10x24 panels, plus hinges or a slider track
  • One pre-hung exterior door, or build a simple plywood door with three hinges and a latch
  • Black weed-barrier fabric and a roll of indoor-outdoor carpet for the floor (sound-deadening)

The Build, Step by Step

1. Frame the floor first, on the ground

Build the floor as a 5x5 square box with 2x6 joists 16 inches on center. Sheath it with the 3/4-inch pressure-treated plywood. Drill holes through the corners now for the carriage bolts that will attach the legs - it is far easier with the floor still on sawhorses than 10 feet in the air.

2. Build and stand the tower

Cut your 4x4 legs to length. With two helpers, stand the legs in their holes (or on concrete pads, more on that below), bolt the floor to the top of the legs, and add temporary diagonal braces. Get everything plumb and square before the next step. Add permanent 2x4 cross-bracing in an X pattern on all four sides of the tower below the floor.

3. Frame the walls on the deck

Now that the platform is solid, frame each wall as a separate panel on top of the deck. Use 2x4s with studs 16 inches on center. Frame your window openings into the studs at this stage. It is easier to cut a clean rough opening in a wall lying flat than to chase one through a sheathed wall later.

4. Stand and sheath the walls

Stand each wall up on the deck, fasten it to the floor, then to the adjacent wall. Sheath with 1/2-inch plywood. Cut your window openings through the sheathing using the framed openings as a guide.

5. Roof

Frame a simple shed roof with a 2-inch fall from front to back. Sheath, paper, then shingle or screw down metal. A shed roof beats a peaked roof on small blinds - it is easier, sheds water better, and you have fewer joints to leak.

6. Windows and door

For windows, the cheap and reliable option is plexiglass or acrylic in a slider track. The pricey-but-quiet option is full tilt-out windows from a dedicated blind supplier. If you are bowhunting, plan for at least two windows that can fully open and not just slide partway. Hinge the door so it swings out, and put a sturdy interior latch on it so wind cannot blow it open in the dark.

7. Paint and ladder

Paint the outside in a dull earth tone or basic camo. Avoid black. A flat black blind in the woods looks like a giant cave entrance to a deer. Build the ladder out of 2x4s with treads 10 inches apart, and add a sturdy handrail. Skipping the handrail is how broken ankles happen.

Footings: Concrete Versus Buried Posts

This is the most argued-about decision in a box blind build, and the right answer depends on your soil and your climate.

Concrete piers or precast blocks are the right call in any spot where frost heave is a factor or where the soil holds water. Set the legs on the piers with metal post bases, and the blind floats above ground moisture for decades.

Buried treated posts work well in dry, sandy soil and they get the blind built faster. Dig 36 inches deep, set the posts in gravel for drainage, and tamp tight. In heavy clay this approach can heave and twist the blind a couple of inches over a few winters.

If you cannot decide, go with concrete. The extra two hours of work pays for itself the first time a different blind in the area starts leaning.

Comfort Upgrades Worth Making

A blind you actually want to sit in is a blind you actually hunt. A few small upgrades dramatically change the experience.

  • Insulated walls. An inch of foam board sheathing on the interior cuts wind noise and traps the heat from a small buddy heater. This single upgrade adds maybe 30 dollars and turns a December sit from miserable to comfortable.
  • A real chair, not a folding camp chair. Build or buy a swivel chair you can lock in place. Most missed shots from a blind come from a hunter trying to maneuver in a chair that does not turn quietly.
  • Indoor-outdoor carpet on the floor. Glue or staple it down. Cuts the noise of a dropped phone or a shifting boot to almost nothing.
  • A small shelf and a hook. One pine board on cleats along the back wall is enough for binos, a thermos, and a phone. A coat hook on the door keeps your bibs off the floor.
  • Window covers from the outside. Black mesh stapled on the inside of the window opening lets you see out while the deer cannot see in. Add it before you ever hunt the blind. It is the single biggest reason hunters in tower blinds get busted.

How Long to Brush It In

A new blind, even a perfectly built one, gets noticed by the deer that have been living in those woods for years. Plan to build the blind at least one full month before the season opener, and brush it in immediately.

Pile cut brush against the legs and at the corners, drape camo netting that matches the local foliage, and avoid the rookie move of clearing a wide perimeter around the blind to "open up shooting lanes." Trim only the lanes you actually plan to shoot. The more your blind looks like a slightly weird brushpile and not a freshly built bunker, the faster deer will accept it.

Maintenance That Buys You Decades

One Saturday morning a year in late summer keeps a homemade blind in the rotation almost indefinitely. Walk around it and look for:

  • Loose ladder bolts or stair treads
  • Window seals starting to crack or pull away from the frame
  • Roof shingles lifting at the leading edge
  • Wasp and hornet nests in the eaves (handle these before bow season, not on opening morning)
  • Mice in the floor cavity - a small piece of hardware cloth over any opening keeps them out

Touch up paint every three to four years. Recaulk window frames every two. Replace ladder treads at the first sign of softness. None of this is hard. All of it is easier than building a second blind in ten years because the first one rotted.

A Note on Permits and Property Rules

Before you put a permanent structure in the ground, check the local rules. On private land in most rural counties this is a non-issue. On leased land you almost certainly need written permission from the landowner. On state or federal hunting ground, permanent structures are usually prohibited outright and you should not even start. Even on private land in some townships, a structure above a certain height is treated as a building and might require a permit. Five minutes on the county website saves an unpleasant conversation later.

Build it once, build it right, and you will be hunting from the same blind with your kids and someday your grandkids. That is the part of this project that does not show up in the materials list, but it is the part that matters.
