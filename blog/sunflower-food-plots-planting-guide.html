Sunflower Food Plots: Complete Planting Guide and Timeline

March 31, 2026 · 6 min read

Why Sunflowers Are One of the Best Food Plot Investments You Can Make

If you manage land for hunting or wildlife, sunflowers might be the single most productive thing you can plant per dollar spent. A well-timed sunflower plot pulls in doves during early season in numbers that most hunters never see elsewhere, and the standing seed heads continue drawing deer and turkeys deep into fall and winter. The plants are forgiving, the seed is cheap, and the results are visible from the road.

I have been planting sunflower food plots on properties in the Southeast for years. This guide covers everything from variety selection to post-harvest management, with timing broken down by region so you can plant with confidence regardless of where your land sits.

Choosing the Right Sunflower Variety

For Dove Hunting

Dove hunters want maximum seed production on plants that mature quickly and uniformly. The birds prefer smaller seeds, and they want a field that peaks right as season opens in September. The go-to varieties for dove plots are:

  • Peredovik (Black Oil) — The gold standard for dove fields. High seed density, uniform maturity, small seed size that doves prefer. Plant this if you are serious about dove hunting.
  • Sunbird and Sunking — Purpose-bred food plot varieties that produce multiple seed heads per plant. Good bird numbers late in the season when individual heads have already been picked clean.
  • Eddie's Select — A popular choice in the Deep South, matures a bit later which can extend your dove season into October.

For Deer and Turkey

Deer are less picky about variety, but they are attracted to larger heads with more meat per seed. They will work sunflower fields hard in August and September, then return to clean up knocked-down stalks throughout winter. Turkeys work the same pattern. For deer-focused plots, consider:

  • Mammoth Gray Stripe — Large heads, high oil content, deer will return to these long after the heads drop.
  • ProCut series — Single-stem varieties bred for uniform maturity and large head size. Less branching means seed production is concentrated rather than spread across multiple smaller heads.

For a mixed-use field that pulls doves in early and holds deer through fall, plant 70 percent black oil Peredovik and 30 percent a larger-headed variety. The combination covers all your bases.

Soil Preparation

Sunflowers are far less demanding than most food plot species, but good soil prep still makes a measurable difference in yield. The target is a clean, loose seedbed with decent fertility and a pH between 6.0 and 7.5.

Start with a soil test if you have not run one recently. Extension offices in most states do these cheap or free. A pH below 6.0 will limit germination and growth. If you need to raise pH, apply lime at least 60 days before planting so it has time to work into the soil.

Disk or till the field to break up compaction and incorporate any existing residue. Sunflowers compete well with weeds once established, but heavy weed pressure in the first three weeks after germination can reduce stands significantly. A pre-plant herbicide pass on fields with a known weed problem is worth the time and cost.

Fertilize at planting using a balanced fertilizer — a 13-13-13 or similar at 200 to 300 pounds per acre is a solid starting point. Sunflowers respond well to phosphorus and potassium. Go easy on nitrogen: high nitrogen pushes vegetative growth at the expense of seed production, which is the opposite of what you want.

Planting Timeline by Region

Deep South (Alabama, Mississippi, Georgia, South Carolina, Louisiana)

Soil temperatures need to hit 50 degrees for germination, but you want consistent warmth for good stands. Plant from late April through mid-June for a September dove season opening. A late April planting gives you plants ready to start maturing in late August with peak seed availability right as doves migrate through. If you miss the April window, a June planting will still produce a late September to October field.

Mid-South and Southeast Transition Zone (Tennessee, Arkansas, North Carolina, Virginia)

Target a mid-May to early June planting. Frost risk is mostly gone by mid-May in lower elevations. Sunflowers take 70 to 90 days to maturity depending on variety, so a May 15 planting of an 80-day variety matures around August 3rd. That gives you a ripe field sitting ready for September 1st season openers.

Midwest and Central Plains (Kansas, Missouri, Oklahoma, Illinois)

Plant from late May to mid-June once soil temperatures are reliably above 50 degrees. The shorter summer in northern parts of this zone means earlier planting is important. Dove season in many Midwest states opens September 1st, and you need heads maturing by then. Use a faster-maturing 70 to 75-day variety if you are planting after June 1st.

Northern States and High Elevation

Planting windows get tight the further north you go. In states like Minnesota, Iowa, or the Dakotas, target a June 1 to June 15 planting window. Sunflowers are sensitive to frost, and you will not have the extended fall season that southern states enjoy. Focus on fast-maturing varieties and plan primarily for deer and turkey activity rather than dove hunting, since dove populations are thinner in the north.

Seed Rates and Planting Method

For food plot purposes, plant at a rate of 3 to 5 pounds of seed per acre for dove fields and 5 to 8 pounds per acre for deer plots where you want denser cover and more stand. Dove hunters traditionally leave some soil bare between plants so birds can land and feed on the ground easily.

A drill seeder set to 1 to 1.5 inches deep gives the most consistent stands. Row spacing of 28 to 36 inches works well and makes post-plant cultivation possible if weed pressure is high. If you are broadcasting seed without a drill, increase your seeding rate by 25 percent and make two passes to ensure even distribution, then lightly disk or drag to get seed-to-soil contact.

Maintenance Through the Season

Sunflowers need relatively little attention after establishment. If you see significant weed competition in the first month, a cultivation pass between rows can make a big difference. Most approved herbicides for sunflowers are limited, so mechanical cultivation is often the most practical option.

Watch for deer browsing on young plants. In areas with heavy deer pressure, a four-foot fence around the plot until plants reach three feet tall prevents them from browsing the field bare before you get a stand established. Alternatively, plant enough acreage that the deer cannot eat it all down before the plants outpace them.

Stink bugs and headworms are the primary pest concerns. In severe infestations, they can reduce seed fill significantly. A single insecticide application at early bloom addresses most pest pressure without requiring repeated treatments.

Harvest and Post-Season Management

The best dove fields look rough. You want a portion of the seed heads knocked down onto bare ground before opening day so birds can feed without having to land on stalks. Mowing a strip across the field with a brush hog one to two weeks before season opens is the classic move. The combination of standing heads and knocked-down seed on bare ground is irresistible to doves.

After dove season, let standing plants remain through winter. Deer and turkeys will continue working the field through January and February as long as seed remains. In spring, disk the old stalks under and replant. Sunflowers are heavy feeders, so rotate to a legume like clover or soybeans every two to three years to restore soil fertility naturally.

A two to five-acre sunflower plot done right will give you more quality hunting moments than fields three times the size planted with less attractive species. Get your timing right, use black oil seed for doves, and spend the money on a soil test. The payoff is real.
