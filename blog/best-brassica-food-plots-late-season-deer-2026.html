Best Brassica Food Plots for Late-Season Deer Hunting in 2026

May 16, 2026 · 7 min read

Late season is where most food plot programs fall apart. The corn is gone, the soybeans got hammered in November, and by the time the rut winds down and deer are running on fumes, your property has nothing left to offer. They drift to the neighbor's standing beans or the soft mast bottom three farms over and you watch the season end on cameras of nothing but does at midnight.

Brassicas fix this. Done right, a brassica plot pulls more late-season deer than any other annual food plot type you can put in the ground, and they keep producing into January and February when most of your property is biological dead zone. Here's how to get it right for the 2026 season.

Why brassicas work when nothing else does

The trick with brassicas is the cold. Frost converts the starches in the leaves and bulbs into sugar — literally the same way frost makes parsnips and carrots sweeter. Deer that ignored your brassica plot in October will start hammering it after the first hard freeze in November and December, and they'll keep eating until the plot is dirt.

The other thing brassicas have going for them: tonnage. A good brassica plot produces 4 to 8 tons of forage per acre. That's two to three times what most clover plots produce, and it's all sitting there exactly when deer need calories the most. Bucks come out of the rut down 25% of their body weight. The herd needs feed. Brassicas deliver feed.

Pick the right brassica blend for your property

There is no single "best" brassica. The right blend depends on your soil, your weather, and what you're trying to accomplish. Here's how to think about it.

Forage rape

Forage rape (Dwarf Essex, Pasja, Hunter) is the workhorse. It establishes fast, produces dense leafy growth, and deer love it once it sweetens up. It's the easiest brassica to grow from scratch, handles a wide range of soils, and forgives planting mistakes. If you're new to brassicas, plant rape.

Turnips

Turnips give you a two-layer food source — leaves above ground that deer eat through fall, then bulbs that deer dig and eat in late winter when nothing else is left. Purple Top, Appin, and T-Raptor are the standard varieties. Deer often have to learn to eat the bulbs (it takes a season or two on a new property) but once they figure it out, turnips become a late-January magnet.

Radishes

Daikon-type forage radishes (Tillage, Groundhog) put down deep taproots that break up compaction and pull nutrients up from the subsoil. Deer eat the tops in fall and the roots in late winter. The real value of radishes is what they do to your soil — plant them in a rotation and your next year's plot will outperform.

Kale and collards

Kale (Bayou, Maris Kestrel) is the highest-tonnage brassica you can plant and the most cold-tolerant. It will keep producing edible forage through subzero weather while turnip tops are dead and gone. The downside: it's expensive seed and slower to establish. Reserve it for high-pressure late-season plots where you really need February groceries.

The blend approach

Don't pick one. Plant a mix. A solid late-season blend looks like 4 lbs/ac of rape, 2 lbs/ac of turnips, 2 lbs/ac of radish, and 1 lb/ac of kale. Deer get fall leaves, December bulbs, January taproots, and February kale. You're feeding the herd from October to spring greenup with one planting.

Timing: when to put seed in the ground

This is where most plots fail. Brassicas need 60 to 80 days of growing weather before the first hard frost to reach their potential. Plant too early and weeds choke them out and they bolt to flower before deer use them. Plant too late and they don't reach productive size before cold weather shuts down growth.

The rule of thumb that actually works: count back 75 days from your average first frost and plant in that two-week window.

  • Northern Midwest, New England, Mountain West: Plant late July to early August.
  • Central Midwest, Mid-Atlantic, Mountain South: Plant early to mid August.
  • Mid-South (Tennessee, Kentucky, Virginia, North Carolina): Plant late August to early September.
  • Deep South (Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Texas): Plant mid to late September.

Plant just before a rain if you can. Brassica seed is tiny — it needs moisture to germinate but it shouldn't be buried more than a quarter inch deep. If you plant into dust and don't get rain in the next 10 days, you've wasted your money.

The four-step planting process that actually produces tonnage

Step 1: Soil test in spring

This is non-negotiable. Brassicas need a pH between 6.0 and 6.8 to perform. If your soil pH is below 6.0, brassicas will look stunted and yellow all season and deer will largely ignore them. Pull soil samples in March or April, send them to your state extension lab ($15 per sample), and follow the lime recommendation. Lime takes 6 months to fully react, which is why you do this in spring for a fall plot.

Step 2: Spray, don't till

If you can spray with glyphosate three weeks before planting, do that instead of tilling. Tilling brings new weed seed to the surface, dries out the soil, and destroys soil structure. A no-till brassica plot in a sprayed seedbed outyields a tilled plot in most years, with a fraction of the work.

If you have to till — because you're breaking new ground or you have established sod — make sure you firm the seedbed before planting. A cultipacker or a heavy roller is ideal. If you don't have one, drag a piece of chain-link fence with a tractor or ATV.

Step 3: Broadcast at the right rate and don't bury the seed

Total seeding rate for a brassica blend should land around 8 to 10 pounds per acre. More seed does not mean more food — overseeding produces a thick mat of thin, stunted plants. Less competition produces bigger leaves and bigger bulbs.

Spread with a hand-crank broadcaster (Earthway or Solo work fine) or an ATV-mounted spreader. After spreading, drag or roll the plot lightly to press seed into soil contact. Do not disk the seed in — brassica seed planted deeper than a half inch struggles to emerge.

Step 4: Fertilize at planting and again at 30 days

Brassicas are heavy nitrogen feeders. The biggest difference between a magazine-cover brassica plot and a sickly one is nitrogen. Apply 200-300 pounds per acre of a balanced fertilizer like 19-19-19 at planting, and come back at 30 days with 100-150 pounds per acre of urea (46-0-0) for a second nitrogen shot. Plots that get that second nitrogen application will produce twice the tonnage of plots that don't.

Hunting strategy: don't burn your brassica plot

The biggest mistake hunters make with brassicas: they hunt them too early. Deer often don't shift onto brassicas heavily until the second or third hard frost has sweetened the leaves. If you hunt the plot in early November before deer are even using it, you spread human scent and risk educating the few deer that do come in.

Better approach: get cameras on the plot the first week of October and watch for the shift. When does and young deer start hitting the plot at daylight and last light, that's your green light. In most of the country that happens in the back half of November.

For a true late-season pattern, hold off entering the plot until the last 10 days of season. Bucks that survived the rut are looking for two things: calories and security. A brassica plot a half mile from a bedding area, with a quiet entry route from the downwind side, will produce afternoon sits where mature deer feed in daylight. Don't waste those sits in October when corn is still standing.

Common reasons brassica plots fail

If your brassica plot didn't perform last year, the cause is almost always one of these:

  • pH too low. The plot looked yellow and stunted all season. Fix: lime now.
  • Planted too late. Plants never got bigger than a beer can. Fix: plant 2 weeks earlier this year.
  • Planted too deep. Spotty germination, bare patches. Fix: surface broadcast and roll, don't disk.
  • Not enough nitrogen. Small leaves, tiny bulbs. Fix: top dress with urea at 30 days.
  • Overgrazed in October. Plot got hammered before frost and never recovered. Fix: plant more acreage or fence off the plot with electric until November.

One more thing: rotate your brassicas

Brassicas are heavy soil feeders and you'll get root maggots and clubroot if you plant the same field in brassicas year after year. Rotate every other year — brassicas one year, a clover-and-cereal-grain mix the next, then back to brassicas. Your tonnage will hold up, your soil will improve, and you'll dodge the disease pressure that wrecks third-year monoculture plots.

Get the brassicas in the ground at the right time, hit them with nitrogen, and let the frost do the work. Come December your phone will be lighting up with trail cam pictures, and your late-season sits will look completely different than they did last year.
